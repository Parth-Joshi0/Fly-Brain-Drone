Whitepaper · Overview

FuityFly: flying a drone with a fruit fly's brain

We took the wiring diagram of a real Drosophila brain, simulated the neurons a fly uses to escape an approaching threat, and gave them control of a DJI Tello quadcopter.

Akshin Makkar, Parth Joshi, Amal Chalayil Sreekumar September 2026
Source code Neural circuit Flight results

1Motivation

In 2023, the FlyWire consortium finished mapping every neuron and synapse in an adult fruit fly brain[2]. Shortly after, Shiu et al. showed that a simple leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model built directly from that wiring diagram could predict real sensorimotor behaviour, with no training involved[1].

That raised a question we wanted to answer with hardware: can connectome-derived circuits act as the controller for a real robot? Drones are a natural fit. A fruit fly is itself a small, fast flyer, and one of its best-studied reflexes is escaping objects that loom towards it.

FuityFly is our answer. It is not a neural network we trained to fly. The circuit that decides when to dodge is a slice of the real connectome: 274 identified neurons connected by the synapses FlyWire recorded between them.

Key idea. No weights were learned. Connectivity comes from the connectome, and neuron dynamics come from the published LIF model. Our engineering work was the interface around the circuit: turning camera frames into the input the fly's visual neurons expect, and turning descending-neuron spikes into drone commands.

2System architecture

The same pipeline runs in simulation and on the real drone. Only the bottom layer, the drone interface, changes.

  1. Vision. Dense optic flow turns each frame into how fast the image is expanding in the left, centre and right of view. Expansion is roughly 2 divided by time-to-contact.
  2. Brain. Expansion drives Poisson spike input into 268 looming-sensitive visual projection neurons (LC4, LPLC2). The circuit is simulated for 20 ms per decision.
  3. Readout. Firing rates of six descending neurons become three signals: escape, yaw and forward.
  4. Action. A controller turns those signals into CRUISE, AVOID or a committed ESCAPE manoeuvre. A safety layer enforces limits and boundaries before commands reach the drone.

3Two behaviours

BEHAVIOUR A

Looming escape

When something approaches fast, the Giant Fiber pathway (DNp01) fires and the drone commits to a roughly 1 s sideways and backward dodge, as a fly jumps away from a swatter.

BEHAVIOUR B

Hunger & feeding

A custom vision model finds bananas and grades their ripeness. A "hunger" drive decays while a banana stays in view, and the drone hovers, "feeds", then lands.

4Results so far

Our most complete real-world test is a 60-second autonomous flight on the physical Tello with the fly-brain controller in the loop. Escapes were triggered by swatting at the drone by hand.

 02

Fly-brain neural pathways

The LC4/LPLC2 → DNp01/03/06 looming circuit, LIF dynamics, and how spikes become commands.

DJI Tello SDK integration

djitellopy, RC control, the video pipeline, unit conversion, and the safety systems for real flight.

PyBullet simulation

A 240 Hz physics sandbox with a PID flight stack and an obstacle course, used to develop the brain safely.

6Limitations & future work

  • Hand-tuned readout. The saturation constants that map spike rates to commands (42, 60 and 30 Hz) were tuned by hand, not derived from fly physiology.
  • A slice of the brain, not the whole brain. We simulate a 274-neuron looming circuit, not the full connectome, to fit the real-time budget.
  • No GPS or position sensing. On the Tello, position is dead-reckoned from commanded velocity.
  • Evaluation. We have not yet run systematic, scored trials in simulation or on hardware. That is the next milestone.
  • Separate behaviours. Hunger and feeding is currently a hand-written state machine rather than a connectome circuit.
