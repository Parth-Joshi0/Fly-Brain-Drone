Part 02 · Neural pathways

Wiring a fly's escape circuit into a drone

We cut a 274-neuron looming-escape circuit out of the FlyWire connectome, simulated it as spiking neurons in real time, and read its descending neurons out as flight commands.

Akshin Makkar, Parth Joshi, Amal Chalayil Sreekumar connectome_worker.py · NeuralPathways/flybrain_controller.py

2.1Why a slice, not the whole brain?

The whole-brain model of Shiu et al.[1] covers roughly 130k neurons. It is built for offline experiments: its batch runner simulates 30 one-second trials and takes minutes. A drone needs an answer to "what should I do about the last 20 ms?" about 30 times a second.

So instead we build one small Brian2 network once, using only the real synapses among a chosen set of neurons, and keep advancing that live network in 20 ms windows. The LIF equations and constants are identical to the full model. What changes is scope: a narrow but genuine slice of the connectome, not a hand-built toy.

2.2The circuit

We chose cell types with well-documented roles in looming detection and escape. We found their FlyWire root IDs through flyconnectome/flywire_annotations and cross-checked them against the v630 completeness list in the repo.

2.3Neuron model

Every neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit with a conductance-like synaptic variable \(g\). These are the same equations as the whole-brain model[1], run in Brian2[3]:

$$\frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{V_0 - v + g}{\tau_m}, \qquad \frac{dg}{dt} = -\frac{g}{\tau}$$(1)

When \(v > V_{th}\) the neuron spikes: \(v \leftarrow V_{rst}\), \(g \leftarrow 0\), and it stays refractory for \(t_{rfc}\). After a delay \(t_{dly}\), each spike adds a signed weight to the postsynaptic neuron:

$$g_j \leftarrow g_j + W_{syn}\cdot w_{ij}$$(2)

Here \(w_{ij}\) is FlyWire's Excitatory × Connectivity value for the pair: the synapse count, signed by the presynaptic neurotransmitter.

2.4From pixels to spikes

The fly's LC4 and LPLC2 neurons respond to images that expand, the visual signature of something on a collision course. We compute the same quantity from the drone's camera:

  1. Dense optic flow (Farnebäck) between consecutive frames.
  2. Rotation removal: the drone's known change in orientation is undone with a homography, so turning is not mistaken for looming.
  3. Expansion is measured as flow divergence in the left, centre and right columns of the image, then median-filtered and smoothed. For an approaching object, expansion \(E \approx 2/\text{TTC}\), where TTC is time-to-contact.

Expansion becomes a loom strength in \([0, 1]\) for each eye. A centred threat feeds both eyes: \(E_L = \max(E_{left}, E_{centre})\), and likewise for the right.

$$\text{loom} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{E - E_{floor}}{2.0},\ 0,\ 1\right), \qquad r_{poi} = \text{loom}\cdot 30\,\text{Hz}$$(3)

\(E_{floor}\) starts at 0.8 s⁻¹ and rises with forward speed and recent rotation. This works like an efference copy: the ground ahead really does expand during forward flight, and optic-flow error grows during fast turns. Each of the 268 input neurons gets its own independent Poisson spike train at rate \(r_{poi}\), and each Poisson spike kicks the membrane by \(W_{syn}F_{poi} = 68.75\) mV, which is individually suprathreshold.

Design decision · 30 Hz, not 150–250 Hz. The whole-brain model's default Poisson rate is 150–250 Hz. At that drive, 268 inputs converging on 6 outputs saturated the escape signal past 0.4 from ordinary cruising flow alone (loom ≈ 0.05). We swept 10, 20, 30 and 50 Hz and chose 30 Hz, so the circuit stays quiet while cruising and engages only at danger-level flow.

2.5From spikes to commands

Each step simulates 20 ms of brain time. A single real neuron fires only 0–4 times in that window, which would make a jumpy 0/50/100/150 Hz staircase. So spike counts are converted to rates and smoothed with an exponential moving average (\(\alpha = 0.25\)). Three signals are then read out:

$$\text{escape} = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{r_{01}^{L} + r_{01}^{R}}{2\cdot 42}\right)$$(4)
$$\text{yaw} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{r_{06}^{R} - r_{06}^{L}}{60},\ -1,\ 1\right)$$(5)
$$\text{forward} = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{r_{03}^{L} + r_{03}^{R} + r_{06}^{L} + r_{06}^{R}}{2\cdot 30}\right)$$(6)

Positive yaw means turn left, following the project's convention. The code is a direct transcription:

connectome_worker.py · step()
escape_rate = rate_of("DNp01", "left") + rate_of("DNp01", "right")
escape = min(1.0, escape_rate / (2 * ESCAPE_SAT_HZ))

yaw_rate = rate_of("DNp06", "right") - rate_of("DNp06", "left")
yaw = max(-1.0, min(1.0, yaw_rate / YAW_SAT_HZ))

forward_drive = rate_of("DNp03", "left") + rate_of("DNp03", "right") \
    + rate_of("DNp06", "left") + rate_of("DNp06", "right")
forward = max(0.0, 1.0 - forward_drive / (2 * FORWARD_SAT_HZ))

The controller then picks a state. If escape ≥ 0.6 it triggers ESCAPE. Otherwise, if |yaw| > 0.05, it enters AVOID_LEFT or AVOID_RIGHT. Otherwise it stays in CRUISE. Outside ESCAPE, the command is:

$$v_{fwd} = 1.0\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\cdot\text{forward}\cdot(1-\text{escape}), \qquad \omega_{yaw} = 0.9\,\tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{s}}\cdot\text{yaw}$$(7)

2.6The escape program

A real Giant Fiber jump is a committed, fixed motor program. It is not re-decided every millisecond, and our escape works the same way:

  1. Cruise. Flow expansion sits below the floor, so loom ≈ 0 and the circuit is quiet.
  2. Approach. An object's expansion (≈ 2/TTC) rises above the floor and the Poisson drive onto LC4/LPLC2 increases.
  3. Trigger. Activity propagates through the real synapses, DNp01 fires, and escape crosses 0.6.
  4. Escape. The drone commits for 30 cycles (≈ 1 s at 30 Hz): a 2.0 m/s sideways strafe plus a 2.0 m/s back-off, pushing about 0.4 m each way before braking.
  5. Refractory. A 30-cycle cooldown follows, so one threat cannot retrigger a string of dodges.

Dodge direction comes from the DNp06 yaw sign when |yaw| ≥ 0.3. Otherwise the drone dodges toward the side with less optic flow, and on a tie it alternates.

Why back away too?. Sideways alone was too slow for the simulated drone: about 1 s to move 0.6 m. In development runs it was hit by a second box in 3 of 3 trials. Adding a backward component brought that to 0 of 3. Raising the back-off speed from 1.0 to 2.0 m/s increased miss distance from 0.12 m to 0.42 m. Pitch and roll saturate independently, so backing off costs no sideways speed.

2.7Runtime architecture

Brian2 is deliberately kept out of the main Python environment: the physics and flight code has no business linking against a spiking simulator. The brain runs as a persistent subprocess in its own conda environment, and the controller exchanges one JSON line per decision over stdin/stdout:

stdin / stdout · JSON lines
// controller → brain
{"loom_left": 0.12, "loom_right": 0.47}
// brain → controller
{"yaw": ..., "forward": ..., "escape": ..., "spike_counts": {"DNp01_left": ..., ...}}
20 msbrain time per step
~20 msmeasured wall time per step (conda env)
33 msbudget at a 30 Hz decision loop

A stray non-conda Brian2 install measured 47 ms per step, over budget. Source: Testing/README.md.

2.8Real-flight evidence

In a 60-second autonomous flight on the physical Tello (74% battery at takeoff, 1,560 processed frames), the controller recorded 19 escape events. Dodges alternated left and right, and expansion readings spanned 0.1–5.7 s⁻¹. Frames per decision state:

StateFrames
CRUISE544
ESCAPE570
AVOID_LEFT266
AVOID_RIGHT108

Source: Testing/tello_escape_flight_test.log. Escapes were triggered by hand swats with no ground-truth scoring, so treat this as evidence that the loop works on hardware, not as an accuracy figure.

2.9Caveats

  • The saturation constants (42 / 60 / 30 Hz), the 30 Hz Poisson ceiling and the loom floor are hand-tuned engineering choices, not physiological constants.
  • "Escape" is read as an evasive manoeuvre, not a literal jump-takeoff.
  • Boundary return and stuck detection are handled by conventional code outside the brain.
  • 274 of roughly 130k neurons: the circuit has no recurrent context from the rest of the brain.
